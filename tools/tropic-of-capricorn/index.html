Direct Answer & Core Functionality

The Tropic of Capricorn is the approximate southern latitude limit where the Sun can appear directly overhead at solar noon. It is commonly shown near 23.5° south and is associated with the Sun’s southernmost subsolar position around the December solstice.

Tropic of Capricorn reference and map accuracy

An approximate reference for the southern tropic

This map uses 23.4364° S as a fixed cartographic reference for the Tropic of Capricorn. The astronomical tropic follows changes in Earth’s axial tilt, so this is not a date-specific solar calculation. Its WGS 84 parallel length is a model-derived reference, not a ground-route measurement. The displayed line and basemap are for geographic reference, not surveying or legal boundary work.

Understanding Your Results & Practical Interpretation

The latitude is a fixed approximation

This interactive map uses 23.4364° S, often rounded in educational maps to about 23.5° S or 23°27′ S. The astronomical Tropic follows changes in Earth’s axial tilt, so this fixed line is for general map reference rather than precision astronomy.

Why the December solstice matters

Around the December solstice, the Sun’s subsolar point reaches its southern annual extreme near the Tropic of Capricorn. This is summer in the Southern Hemisphere and winter in the Northern Hemisphere.

Countries commonly listed on the line

A conventional country list names ten crossings: Chile, Argentina, Paraguay, Brazil, Namibia, Botswana, South Africa, Mozambique, Madagascar, and Australia. Counts can depend on boundary data and on whether a line touching a border or island is counted.

Mathematical Methodology & Geodetic Accuracy

Approximate Tropic Latitude and WGS 84 Parallel Length

The map uses 23.4364° S as a static, commonly used cartographic reference. The astronomical tropic is related to Earth’s obliquity and is not a permanently fixed latitude. Parallel length is calculated on the WGS 84 ellipsoid.

φref = 23.4364° S; N = a / √(1 − e² sin²φ); C = 2πN cosφ

Troubleshooting & Geographic Edge Cases

Why do maps show 23.5°, 23°27′, or 23.4364° S?

These are rounded or conventional map reference values. Earth’s astronomical Tropic is related to axial tilt and is not an immutable exact latitude; this page documents the fixed value it uses.

Does the map show the Sun’s exact position today?

No. It is a static reference parallel. Computing a date-specific subsolar point requires astronomical calculations that change with date and time.

Why do sources list different countries?

Crossing counts may differ because of map scale, boundary datasets, territorial or island conventions, and whether a boundary contact counts as crossing a country.
